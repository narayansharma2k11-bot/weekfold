Navi Pillay, champion of international justice, wins Nobel Peace Prize

The 85-year-old South African jurist was honoured for decades of work making sure war crimes and genocide are prosecuted.

The Norwegian Nobel Committee on Friday awarded the 2026 Nobel Peace Prize to Navanethem "Navi" Pillay, a South African judge of Indian Tamil descent, for her work promoting peace and international law. Committee chair Jørgen Watne Frydnes said she had been instrumental in bringing war crimes, crimes against humanity and genocide to court. The committee said international law has become an absolute necessity at a time when its institutions are under attack.

Pillay, 85, opened Natal's first law practice run by a woman in 1967 and defended Nelson Mandela and other anti-apartheid activists. She became the first woman of colour on South Africa's High Court, then served on the Rwanda genocide tribunal, where judges established that rape could be an act of genocide. She later sat at the International Criminal Court, served as UN High Commissioner for Human Rights, and now sits on the International Court of Justice in the Rohingya genocide case against Myanmar.

More recently she chaired the UN commission of inquiry on the occupied Palestinian territory, which concluded in 2025 that Israel was committing genocide in Gaza. Israel's foreign ministry condemned the award as legitimising anti-Israel prejudice. UN Secretary-General António Guterres and South African President Cyril Ramaphosa welcomed it.

The choice is widely seen as a rebuke to Washington, which has sanctioned ICC officials and, hours after the announcement, sanctioned the court itself. President Donald Trump, who openly wanted the prize, said the committee had demeaned it. Speaking in Nuremberg, Pillay dedicated the award to survivors of international crimes and joked that she might share it with Trump.

The prize, worth 12 million Swedish crowns (about $1.2 million), will be presented in Oslo on 10 December. The committee received 287 nominations this year.

Why it matters

The award puts the Nobel's weight behind war-crimes courts at a moment when major powers are openly attacking them.
