IceCube founder Francis Halzen wins Nobel Prize in Physics

The Belgian-born physicist is honoured for leading the IceCube Neutrino Observatory at the South Pole and for detecting high-energy neutrinos from deep space.

The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences on Tuesday awarded the 2026 Nobel Prize in Physics to Francis Halzen, 82, for decisive contributions to the IceCube Neutrino Observatory and for the discovery of high-energy neutrinos from astrophysical sources. Halzen was born in Belgium and has been on the physics faculty of the University of Wisconsin–Madison since 1972. Wisconsin–Madison is IceCube's lead institution. He receives the full prize of 12 million Swedish kronor, about $1.2 million.

Neutrinos are nearly massless particles that pour out of stars, including the Sun, and trillions pass through every person each second. They almost never interact with matter, so they cannot be seen directly. Scientists instead look for the faint flashes of light produced on the rare occasions they collide with atoms. In 1988 Halzen proposed using the clear, deep ice at the South Pole as a giant detector. IceCube, completed in 2011, watches about one cubic kilometre of ice with thousands of sensors buried nearly a mile down.

The observatory has produced a run of firsts. In 2013 it detected high-energy neutrinos from beyond the solar system. In 2017 it traced one to a distant galaxy powered by a supermassive black hole, helping to identify a source of cosmic rays. In 2023 it produced the first image of the Milky Way made with neutrinos instead of light.

Mark Pearce, chair of the Nobel Committee for Physics, said Halzen had led an international team that gave science a fantastic instrument. Speaking by phone from Italy, Halzen called the news a great surprise. He said that even though people had predicted he would win, the moment still felt strange.

IceCube was expanded early this year with several hundred new sensors. Researchers hope the extra sensitivity will help them work out where the universe's most energetic particles come from.

Why it matters

Neutrino astronomy offers a new way to study black holes and the most violent events in the universe, which light-based telescopes cannot see.
