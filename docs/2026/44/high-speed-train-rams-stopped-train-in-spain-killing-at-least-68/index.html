High-speed train rams stopped train in Spain, killing at least 68

A state-run Renfe train struck the rear of a stopped Iryo train south of Madrid; the transport minister called the order to restart it inexplicable.

At least 68 people died on Wednesday morning when one high-speed train slammed into the back of another that was stopped on the tracks in central Spain, in the country's second deadly high-speed rail disaster this year. The collision happened at about 8:53 a.m. on the Madrid-Seville line in Toledo province, on a flat stretch between Urda and Los Yébenes, surrounded by olive fields. Two carriages derailed. The regional government of Castilla-La Mancha confirmed the toll, and 58 injured people were being treated in hospital, with AFP reporting that more than 100 were hurt and 15 were in a critical condition.

A train run by state-owned Renfe, carrying 397 passengers, hit a train of the private operator Iryo carrying 147. Transport Minister Óscar Puente said a safety system on a stretch of about 63 kilometres failed at 8:30 a.m. because of a computer malfunction, halting all trains there. Central command then told the Renfe train to restart without speed restrictions even though a stop signal remained active. He called the order inexplicable, said the drivers were not to blame, and said the train could have been travelling at close to 200 km/h. He added that he was not assigning responsibility to anyone and was only stating facts.

Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez, speaking at the site, announced three days of national mourning and said it was unacceptable for Spain to suffer two high-speed rail accidents in one year. King Felipe VI visited the scene, and European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen said Europe mourned with Spain. The football federation postponed all 24 Copa del Rey games scheduled for the day, and the main Madrid-Andalusia line was suspended.

The crash echoes January's disaster near Adamuz in southern Spain, when an Iryo train derailed into an oncoming Renfe train and 46 people died. Before 2026, no fatal crash had occurred on Spain's dedicated high-speed lines since the network opened in 1992, AP noted. A union spokesperson said liberalisation of passenger services may not have been matched by upkeep of the lines, though he stopped short of calling it decisive.

Authorities are investigating, and the toll was expected to rise. Sánchez faces an early general election on 29 November, and both the Socialists and opposition leader Alberto Núñez Feijóo suspended campaigning.

Why it matters

Two fatal high-speed crashes in ten months, one now blamed on a control-centre order, put Spain's rail safety and its rail liberalisation under intense scrutiny weeks before an election.
