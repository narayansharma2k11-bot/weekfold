Studies find global warming is accelerating, with seven climate records broken

Researchers say the warming rate has almost doubled over the past decade, and a separate annual report finds seven of 16 vital signs at record extremes.

Two studies published this month point to a planet warming faster than before. In Geophysical Research Letters, Grant Foster and Stefan Rahmstorf of the Potsdam Institute for Climate Impact Research found that global temperatures have risen by about 0.35 degrees Celsius per decade over the past 10 years, nearly twice the just-under-0.2 degrees per decade seen from 1970 to 2015. ScienceDaily, which carried the institute's release, said the acceleration is statistically significant at more than 98 percent confidence.

The team analysed five major datasets, from NASA, NOAA, HadCRUT, Berkeley Earth and ERA5, after removing natural influences such as El Niño. Signs of acceleration appear in all five from around 2013 or 2014, and 2023 and 2024 remain the hottest years on record even after adjustment. If the past decade's rate continues, the researchers say, the 1.5 degree Paris Agreement limit could be exceeded on a long-term basis before 2030.

A separate report, the seventh annual State of the Climate analysis in the journal BioScience, looked at 16 planetary vital signs and found seven at record extremes: sea level, ocean heat content, glacier thickness, Greenland ice mass, and the three main greenhouse gases. Eos, the American Geophysical Union's news outlet, reported that carbon dioxide passed 428 parts per million in May 2026, about 50 percent above pre-industrial levels, and that the warming rate has roughly doubled from about 0.19 degrees per decade in 1970 to 2010 to about 0.36 since 2010.

There is some good news. The report says global oil, coal and gas use rose 0.9 percent in 2025, but renewables overtook coal in global electricity generation that year. Co-author William Ripple, of Oregon State University, warned that continued warming could trigger additional feedbacks and tipping points, including a slowdown of the Atlantic overturning circulation, Amazon dieback and ice-sheet melt in Greenland and West Antarctica.

The two analyses use slightly different baselines, which explains the small gaps in their decadal rates.

Why it matters

If warming has indeed sped up, the window for limiting it to 1.5 degrees is closing faster than most plans assumed.
