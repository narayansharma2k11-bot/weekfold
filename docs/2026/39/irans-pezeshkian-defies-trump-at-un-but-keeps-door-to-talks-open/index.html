Iran's Pezeshkian defies Trump at UN but keeps door to talks open

A day after Trump's threats, Iran's president told world leaders his country was a victim of terrorism, as US envoys and Iranian officials held their first contact in weeks.

Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian told the UN General Assembly on Wednesday that Iran had been the victim of terrorism, not its sponsor, responding directly to Donald Trump's threats the day before. He held up photos of children killed when a missile hit a school in Minab on the first day of the war, a strike that killed 156 people, and an image of the late Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei, killed in the opening attacks. The US delegation walked out.

Pezeshkian said Iran wanted military strength only for self-defence, rejected the idea that nuclear knowledge should belong to a few nations, and said the dispute had no military solution. He said Iran would not surrender its navigation rights in the Strait of Hormuz. He ended by saying Tehran was ready for negotiations, though not under threat of force.

Behind the speeches, diplomacy stirred. On Tuesday, US envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner held about three hours of talks with an Iranian team led by Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi, with Qatar mediating. Witkoff called the round constructive and promising, and Iranian state media described it as the first direct contact in weeks. Iran's conditions include lifting the US naval blockade, releasing frozen assets and ending the war on all fronts.

Reactions were guarded. Secretary of State Marco Rubio dismissed Pezeshkian's human rights claims and warned against calling the talks a breakthrough. Iran's foreign ministry spokesman said the meeting produced nothing new. Iran's military warned of crushing blows if attacked again.

Euronews noted that Pezeshkian's claim that Iran had not targeted civilians is contradicted by documented deaths of sailors and civilians in Iranian attacks across the region. Qatar and Pakistan are continuing mediation efforts.

Why it matters

Any opening between Washington and Tehran could decide whether the Hormuz shipping crisis eases or the war escalates again, with global energy markets in the balance.
