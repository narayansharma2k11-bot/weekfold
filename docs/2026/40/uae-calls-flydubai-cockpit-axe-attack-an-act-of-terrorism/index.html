UAE calls FlyDubai cockpit axe attack an act of terrorism

A co-pilot struck the captain with the crash axe on a Dubai-Tel Aviv flight; passengers subdued him and the jet landed safely in Saudi Arabia.

The United Arab Emirates, which is leading the investigation, said a FlyDubai co-pilot attacked his captain with the cockpit's crash axe and tried to take control of the aircraft on flight FZ1073 from Dubai to Tel Aviv. UAE Prosecutor General Hamad Saif al-Shamsi said the co-pilot had attempted a terrorist act. The incident took place on Wednesday 30 September; the UAE gave details on 3 October.

The plane, carrying 182 people, most of them Israeli, plunged roughly 17,000 feet, steeply enough that part of the rudder was torn away. The captain, Indian pilot Smit Machchhar, was badly wounded but managed to unlock the cockpit door. Passengers and off-duty crew overpowered the co-pilot, and two other pilots on board stabilised the jet, which made an emergency landing in Tabuk, northwestern Saudi Arabia. Saudi authorities arrested the attacker and handed him to the UAE.

People familiar with the case identified the co-pilot as Hamam al-Hammami, a UAE-born Omani national. Oman Air had reportedly grounded him over concerns about extremist views before FlyDubai hired him; a Gulf diplomat said he passed standard checks. Officials had not formally confirmed his identity. Investigators from four regional states, helped by the US and others, have so far found no links to militant groups.

Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu said Israel was examining whether anyone directed the attack and praised the captain for saving many lives. FlyDubai suspended its Israel route and El Al cancelled flights, stranding some travellers until repatriation flights began. Questions remain over how a pilot from a country without ties to Israel was rostered on the route.

The case has prompted calls to tighten cross-border background checks on pilots, which vary widely between countries.

Why it matters

An attack from inside the cockpit exposes gaps in airline crew vetting worldwide and strains fragile Israel-Gulf air links.
