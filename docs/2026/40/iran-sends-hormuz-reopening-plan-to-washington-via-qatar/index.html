Iran sends Hormuz reopening plan to Washington via Qatar

Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi met Qatari mediators in New York and said he expected a US answer within a day, while Washington insisted any deal must cover Iran's nuclear programme.

Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi held talks with Qatari mediators in New York, on the sidelines of the UN General Assembly, about Tehran's plan to reopen the Strait of Hormuz. Speaking to Iranian state media late on Monday, he said the Qataris would carry the ideas discussed to Washington and that Tehran hoped for a formal US reply by Tuesday. He described the exchange of messages through Qatari and Pakistani channels as having become more serious.

Iran's proposal, presented at the UN the previous Friday, asks the United States to unfreeze Iranian funds, lift sanctions and end its naval blockade of Iranian ports. Araghchi said those steps could be finished in four or five days, after which the strait would reopen, with talks on a final settlement, including the nuclear file, starting by day seven.

Washington's response was cool. President Donald Trump had called the plan "unacceptable" on Saturday. A US official described the indirect contacts as positive but said there would be no agreement to end the war without progress on Iran's nuclear programme, adding that the president was willing to ease sanctions and release funds in return. The official also accused Iran of breaching a June memorandum by firing on merchant ships; Tehran denies this and says the US failed to restrain Israeli attacks on Lebanon.

Iran shut the strait after the US and Israel launched their offensive on 28 February. Before the war, about a fifth of the world's oil and gas passed through it, and oil has since traded above $100 a barrel. Some tankers now use a US-escorted route along the Omani coast, and Gulf crude exports recovered to 12.8 million barrels a day in September, still well below February's level.

Iranian military leaders struck a defiant tone, saying the strait remains closed. The next test is whether Qatar can bridge the gap over sequencing: Iran wants economic relief first, while Washington wants nuclear commitments up front.

Why it matters

The Strait of Hormuz is the world's most important energy chokepoint, and its closure has kept oil and fuel prices high across the globe.
