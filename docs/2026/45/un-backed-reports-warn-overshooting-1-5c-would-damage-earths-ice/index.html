UN-backed reports warn overshooting 1.5C would damage Earth's ice irreversibly

Scientists say even a temporary breach of the Paris limit could affect glaciers, sea ice and permafrost for centuries.

Two reports released at the start of November warn that exceeding 1.5C of global warming, even temporarily, would cause large-scale and in many cases irreversible damage to the world's frozen regions. One, titled Overshoot and the Cryosphere, was written with more than 80 scientists by the International Cryosphere Climate Initiative with the UN Environment Programme. Its lead author is glaciologist James Kirkham.

AFP reported that warming has reached 1.37C above pre-industrial levels and that Copernicus projects 1.5C could be passed around 2030. The authors say current warming is already unsafe for the Arctic, Antarctic and mountain glaciers, and that a safe level for ice may be near 1C or lower. They cite Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets melting about six times faster than in the 1990s. Above 2.8C, sea level rise could reach up to 5cm a year by 2150, against 0.45cm a year now, and permafrost thaw could release each year about as much carbon dioxide as the United States emits.

Some glaciers and sea ice could partly recover if temperatures fall back, but Matthew England of the University of New South Wales said that could take decades to centuries. The reports urge keeping any overshoot as small and brief as possible and cutting fossil fuel use faster. Kirkham argued that a temporary breach makes the Paris goals more urgent, not irrelevant.

Why it matters

The reports argue that even a short breach of the 1.5C limit would have lasting costs for ice, water supplies and sea levels.
