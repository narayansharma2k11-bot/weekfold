Tankers burn in Hormuz as Iran presses on after US midterms

Iran says mines and fires hit at least five oil tankers and a tugboat on Thursday, even as Qatar and Oman try to keep talks alive.

Iran's military said on Thursday 5 November that at least five oil tankers and a tugboat were damaged in the Strait of Hormuz in a string of incidents. The claims came from Iranian state-linked media and officials, and maritime authorities have not independently confirmed them.

The Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps told the Fars news agency that a large tanker trying to use what it called an illegal passageway south of the strait hit a mine, caught fire and stopped. It said a supertanker leaving the eastern end of the strait was halted by a large blaze, and that a tugboat that approached to help also caught fire. The Iranian army later said three more tankers were hit by naval mines and set alight, one east of the strait early in the day and two around noon.

Separately, the UK Maritime Trade Operations agency reported that a tanker was struck by two projectiles about 50 nautical miles (93 km) north of Madinat ash-Shamal in Qatar, late on Wednesday. It did not name the ship or its flag, and nobody claimed responsibility. The crew's condition was not known. On 30 October, a Qatari LNG carrier was hit by unidentified objects in the Gulf, though no one was hurt and the ship kept operating.

The strait normally carries roughly a fifth of the world's oil. Iran has restricted traffic there since the US-Israel war on it began on 28 February, and the United States has blockaded Iranian ports. Attacks on shipping are running close to a record pace, and the UK monitoring agency has said it struggles to count them because most vessels no longer report incidents.

Markets reacted cautiously. Brent crude had fallen more than 8 percent on Wednesday to about 94 dollars a barrel, its first close below 100 dollars since September, after the US midterm elections. On Thursday it rose 2.2 percent to about 96.70 dollars, according to CBS News.

The political backdrop is tense. President Donald Trump acknowledged the war had partly hurt Republicans at the polls but said he would do it again, and told the Financial Times he had no immediate plans to strike while the US remained locked and loaded. The New York Times, citing unnamed Pentagon officials, reported that thousands of US personnel on more than a dozen ships and at bases are ready to resume major combat if ordered. Iran's Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei dismissed hopes of a change in US policy after the vote and warned that the war would widen if attacks were repeated.

On the diplomatic track, Qatar's Foreign Ministry said both sides were reviewing mediators' proposals, and Oman said it was still working with Iran on a joint mechanism to manage traffic through the strait. Whether those efforts can hold against the mines, the fires and the military buildup is the question for the days ahead.

Why it matters

A fifth of the world's oil passes through Hormuz, so each burning tanker raises the risk of a wider war and higher energy prices just as mediators try to keep talks alive.
