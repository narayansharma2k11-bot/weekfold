Ukraine and Russia emergency alert apps hacked as local truce ends

False nuclear, chemical and missile warnings reached phones on both sides of the front within hours of each other.

Emergency alert apps in both Ukraine and Russia were hit by cyberattacks at the turn of the weekend, pushing false warnings to ordinary users, according to officials from both countries and reports from Reuters, the Associated Press and Al Jazeera.

In Ukraine, the national air raid alert app showed fake notices late on Sunday, 1 November, claiming nuclear and chemical threats around Kyiv and warning of street fighting. One message said Russia had deployed its Oreshnik hypersonic missile. The Ukrainian Air Force said the app appeared to have been subjected to "enemy influence", told residents there was no radiation threat and urged calm. AP reported that the alerts appeared in English, Russian and Ukrainian, that the English versions contained spelling and grammar mistakes, and that the app was briefly suspended and then restored. Air force spokesman Yurii Ihnat said the incident did not affect security or defence operations, and sirens and other warning channels kept working.

Early on Monday, Russia's Emergencies Ministry said its own mobile app had suffered a large cyberattack that spread false information. AP said a false alert went out at about 2:45 a.m. Moscow time and reached 33 of Russia's 89 regions, while the ministry said it had identified the vulnerability and was taking steps to fix it. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said state bodies face constant cyber threats, including some that use artificial intelligence.

Neither government has said who was behind the attack on Russia's app. Ukraine's Centre for Strategic Communications blamed Russia for the hack of the Ukrainian app. AP noted that a Russian Telegram channel linked to the security services credited a hacking group called Hitcore with the Ukrainian breach, a claim AP could not verify. Reuters said it was unclear whether the two incidents were connected.

The incidents came as a rare local truce was ending in the Russian-occupied city of Oleshky in Ukraine's Kherson region. AP said the pause began on 27 October to allow humanitarian aid and evacuations, and described it as the most significant truce between the two sides since the full-scale invasion began; Al Jazeera reported that it ended on Sunday. Ukraine's general staff said Russia used the lull to reinforce drone defences and restock ammunition. Ukrainian ombudsman Dmytro Lubinets, the lead civilian negotiator, had hoped the arrangement could be a model for wider efforts and support three-way talks with Russia and the United States expected this month.

Fighting continued elsewhere. Ukrainian officials said drone strikes injured 12 people in Kharkiv and five in Odesa, while Russia's Defence Ministry said it shot down 212 Ukrainian drones overnight and the governor of Samara said air defences were repelling an attack. In Kherson, regional authorities said a 62-year-old woman and a 73-year-old man were taken to hospital with blast injuries as the truce lapsed.

Why it matters

Alert systems are meant to be trusted in seconds, so tampering with them is a way to sow fear without firing a shot. The episode also shows how fragile the one durable pause in the war was.
